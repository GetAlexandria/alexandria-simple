## WHAT

A tool amplifies a person's thinking only when the connections between its parts are
visible to the person using it — without that visible model of how the pieces relate, the
components do not amplify anything, they are just typed into and read back at, like
speaking into a void that happens to answer. The circuit that turns raw components into an
amplifier is the model of their connections, and that model is exactly what goes missing
when knowledge is stored as isolated facts rather than as a graph of how those facts relate.

## WHY

This lesson matters because it locates where a library's real value actually sits: not in
any single card's content, but in the links between cards, which are the hidden circuit
that makes the whole thing amplify a director's or a colleague's thinking rather than just
store it. It is the reason a library of unconnected atomic facts would fail to deliver what
this product promises, even if every individual fact in it were accurate.

## WHERE

This lesson is why the library's links, not merely its cards, are the load-bearing
part of the substrate great
[[Bet - Colleagues Grown from Company Design|colleagues are grown from]].

It also grounds the choice to hold knowledge as
[[Bet - Traversible Context|traversible context]] — a linked graph a colleague and
a director can move through together, rather than documents to search.

## HOW

The evidence is a desk-research synthesis applying a general theory of cognitive
amplification to this product's specific claim that a library's connections, not its
individual cards, are where the value lives. It establishes the theoretical case that
visible connections are what make a tool amplify rather than merely store; it does not yet
establish how much connection density a library actually needs before this amplifying
effect becomes noticeable to the people using it, or how it degrades if the graph is
sparse or its links go stale. That threshold is what building out and using the library at
real scale would surface.

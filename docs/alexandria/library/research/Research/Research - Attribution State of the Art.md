## WHAT

Determining which of several intertwined causes drove an outcome is a well-worked problem in the applied sciences, and the field's answer is that attribution is a portfolio of instruments, not one technique, ordered by how directly each isolates a cause. When a factor can be split off and randomly assigned, so that otherwise-identical groups differ only in that one respect, a controlled experiment — the online A/B test — is the causal gold standard: the only design that turns a correlation into a defensible claim that this cause, and not some other one, produced the outcome. When a factor cannot be split off this cleanly, because it describes a whole product's shape rather than a switchable feature, the field falls back on a graded ladder of substitutes: coarser-unit randomized experiments, quasi-experimental designs that find or build a counterfactual without randomizing anyone, choice experiments that vary the attribute on a constructed instrument, algorithmic credit-splitting across causes that only ever act together, revealed behavioral preference, survey-based driver analysis, and, at the floor, asking people directly. The resolution a bet deserves should track how subtle its causal question actually is, not how easy the nearest instrument is to run.

## WHY

Holding this evidence buys an honest instrument for a question the product cannot afford to answer by assumption: when adoption rises, which bet earned it. Without a calibrated way to separate causes that move together, the natural failure mode is to credit whichever bet is easiest to observe or loudest to champion — typically the AI colleagues, since they are the visible interaction layer — while the visual, traversible environment underneath either gets over-credited by good storytelling or quietly discounted for lack of proof. A portfolio graded by how directly each method isolates a cause, and by what it costs to run, makes it possible to choose an instrument that matches how subtle the causal question actually is: cheap and directional where the question is already loud and self-evident, more rigorous and expensive where the question is genuinely tangled.

## HOW

The strongest evidence type is a randomized controlled experiment — the online A/B test refined into a discipline of its own by experimentation platforms at large consumer software companies. Two otherwise-identical groups are exposed to different versions of one thing, assignment is random, and any difference in a pre-registered outcome metric is causally attributable to that one thing, because randomization is what rules out every other explanation at once. Run with a single overall evaluation criterion and a short list of guardrail metrics that must not get worse, it is the only design in this ladder that produces a real counterfactual rather than an inferred one, and it is comparatively cheap once a team can split traffic and read the result reliably. Its bind is exactly the one this card exists to answer: it needs a factor that can be switched on for some users and off for others while everything else about their experience stays the same, and a whole-product attribute such as "make it visual" is not that kind of factor — two such builds are not swappable at random for individual sessions without users noticing, learning, or talking to each other about the difference, so the assignment leaks. The same failure mode is documented directly in two-sided marketplaces, where one side's experience contaminates the other's and breaks the assumption that a unit's outcome depends only on its own treatment.

Before leaving randomization behind altogether, the field has one more move within the experimental family itself: change the unit being randomized rather than abandon random assignment. Geo-experiments, developed to measure advertising effects that could not be split user by user, randomize whole geographic regions into treatment and control instead of individual sessions, trading away the large sample sizes of a user-level A/B test for a design that still delivers a true randomized counterfactual; the same move — randomizing by time window, by market, or by cohort instead of by user — is the standard answer whenever a factor cannot be switched on for one user without everyone around them noticing. It remains the gold-standard family, only run at a coarser grain, and is usually worth trying before reaching for a non-experimental substitute.

When even a coarser-grained randomization is not available — there is no set of comparable regions or cohorts to split, or the change has already shipped to everyone — the next rung is quasi-experimental causal inference: methods that infer a causal effect from data the world produced on its own, without an experimenter assigning anything. Difference-in-differences compares a group exposed to a change against a similar group that was not, before and after the change, and credits the gap between their trends to the change; its cost is low if a comparable untreated population already exists, and its main risk is that the two groups must have been moving in parallel beforehand, an assumption that can fail silently. Synthetic control solves the same problem when no single comparable group exists, by building an artificial one as a weighted blend of many untreated candidates that tracked the treated case closely before the change — the closest quasi-experimental match to "Alexandria without the visual environment," constructed from comparable products or comparable cohorts rather than assigned. Regression discontinuity exploits a sharp cutoff — some threshold above which a factor kicks in and below which it does not — and compares outcomes just above and just below the line, on the logic that whoever sits right at the edge is close to randomly assigned; it needs a real threshold to exist and only speaks confidently about behavior near that threshold. Instrumental variables finds some other factor that nudges people toward or away from the thing being tested for reasons unrelated to the outcome itself, and uses that nudge as a stand-in for an assignment that was never randomized; it is powerful when a genuinely unrelated nudge can be found, and close to useless when the best available instrument is only weakly connected to the factor of interest. Propensity-score matching instead balances a comparison statistically, pairing adopters and non-adopters who look alike on every observable trait and reading the outcome gap between matched pairs as the effect, which is cheap to run against data already collected but is only as good as the traits it was able to observe and match on. None of these five needs an experimenter to assign anything, which is exactly why they answer a bet that cannot be switched on and off at will; what they cost in return is a design-specific assumption that has to be checked rather than guaranteed by randomization.

A different answer to the same bind is to stop trying to vary the whole product and instead vary the attribute on a controlled instrument built for exactly that purpose. Conjoint analysis shows people a series of product profiles that mix attributes — including how visual or traversible a working environment is — at different levels, and infers from their ratings or choices how much weight each attribute carried in the decision, decomposing a bundle nobody could split apart live. Discrete-choice modeling grounds that same choice data in a utility-maximizing model of behavior, so the resulting attribute weights translate into defensible predictions about market share or willingness to pay rather than just a ranking. MaxDiff, or best-worst scaling, is a cheaper cousin of the same idea: instead of rating every attribute as important, respondents repeatedly pick the best and worst of a small set, which forces real trade-offs and resists the tendency of survey respondents to call everything a priority. All three need a real instrument to build and a real respondent pool to run, and all three are stated behavior on a constructed task rather than observed behavior on the live product, but they are the field's purpose-built way to isolate the value of one attribute inside a whole-product bundle that a live experiment cannot split.

When a factor cannot be isolated even on a constructed instrument, because it is entangled with everything else in a system that is already live and already working, the field turns to algorithmic credit-splitting across causes that only ever act in combination. The Shapley value, a solution concept from cooperative game theory, treats each candidate cause as a player and assigns it the average of its marginal contribution across every possible order in which the causes could have been added; it is the only allocation that satisfies a small set of fairness axioms and guarantees the credit assigned to every cause sums exactly to the outcome being explained, and it has been carried from game theory into marketing multi-touch attribution and into regression itself, where it corrects the standard problem of correlated causes stealing credit from one another. Markov-chain, or removal-effect, attribution takes a more behavioral route to the same goal: it maps the sequence of touchpoints or features a person actually passes through as a chain of transitions, then asks how much a conversion probability drops when one step is deleted from that chain, crediting each step by how much the outcome would have suffered without it. Both methods need volume — a real, sizeable trail of paths or a real set of co-occurring causes to decompose — and both are silent about causes that never vary at all across the data they are given, so a bet that never ships without another bet beside it cannot be separated by either method until the two are at least occasionally observed apart.

Revealed preference is the cheapest instrument that still counts as behavioral evidence rather than opinion: grounded in the economic theory that a person's actual choice between affordable options reveals their true ranking of them, it reads attribution directly off what people do when given a real choice — do they keep using the visual surface when a plainer path is equally available, do they drop a competing tool once they have this one. It costs little beyond instrumenting the choice itself, and it is the strongest floor evidence available short of an experiment, but it only isolates a cause cleanly when the choice on offer isolates that cause; if choosing the visual surface always also means choosing the colleagues behind it, the behavior cannot tell the two apart any better than plain adoption numbers already do. Survey key-driver analysis asks a comparable question of stated ratings instead of choices: it regresses an overall satisfaction or adoption rating onto ratings of the individual sub-components people experienced, and decomposes the result — often with the same Shapley-value logic used in algorithmic attribution — to keep correlated sub-components from stealing credit from one another unfairly. It is cheap to field and good at surfacing which sub-component a population associates with its overall impression, but it still runs on stated ratings, one step removed from the choice-experiment methods above, and needs enough respondents who experienced the sub-components differently to say anything at all.

At the floor sits direct self-report: asking people why they adopted something, tallying feature requests, reading support conversations for the reason someone gives for switching. It is the fastest and cheapest instrument in the entire portfolio, and the only one that can run before any of the others have data to work with, which makes it good for generating a first hypothesis about which bet is winning. Its long-documented weakness is the say-do gap — the classic demonstration that people's stated attitudes and their actual behavior can diverge sharply, echoed ever since in market research's own finding that the reason people give for a decision is an unreliable guide to what actually drove it — so self-report earns a place at the start of an attribution effort, never at its end.

Read as a whole, the ladder sorts along two axes worth naming: say versus do, and lab versus field. Self-report, survey key-driver analysis, conjoint, and MaxDiff are say evidence, stated on a real or constructed instrument rather than observed in the wild; revealed preference, the experimental family, the quasi-experimental designs, and the algorithmic credit-splitting methods are do evidence, read off behavior that actually happened. Conjoint, discrete-choice modeling, and MaxDiff are also lab evidence in the classic sense, a constructed task standing in for the live product, where the experimental family, revealed preference, and the algorithmic methods are field evidence, drawn from real usage once enough of it exists. Neither axis makes one method strictly better — a lab, say-based conjoint study can isolate an attribute that field, do-based revealed-preference data cannot separate at all, and the reverse holds just as often. The practical answer to a bet that cannot be cleanly assigned at random is triangulation across both axes, trusting a reading most where independent methods agree. None of this, on its own, establishes which of Alexandria's bets is winning; it establishes which instrument to reach for once that question needs answering, and how much weight its answer can bear.

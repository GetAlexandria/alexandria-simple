Research · Tier Framework

Three Tiers

← Research Playbook

How the three tiers — Coordinator, PM, and Sr. Manager — were defined, what differentiates each level, and how ownership shifts as the role grows in scope and authority.

◇
Technical Coordinator
Inputs · runs the machine
Tier 1
The Coordinator is the operational backbone of the product team. They keep the machine running — tracking timelines, maintaining backlogs, running recurring syncs, and ensuring information flows where it needs to go. They produce inputs for the PM to act on; they do not own the decisions.

The defining characteristic is process ownership without authority to decide. The Coordinator owns delivery logistics and communications infrastructure, but the "what to build" and "why" calls live above them.
Primary focus
Delivery operations, status communications, backlog hygiene
Authority ceiling
Inputs and execution logistics — not feature or strategy decisions
Technical layer adds
Technical doc upkeep, dev backlog management, feasibility note capture — literacy, not architecture
What differentiates from PM
Does not own the problem space, the spec, or the launch decision — runs the process that enables the PM to do those things

◆
Technical PM
Owns a feature end-to-end
Tier 2
The PM is the feature owner. They own the complete arc: identify a real problem, define the right solution, get it built, ship it, and measure whether it worked. They hold the spec, the prioritization call for their area, and accountability for the outcome.

What changes from Coordinator: decision authority. The PM owns "what we build and why" for their scope. They are not just producing inputs — they are synthesizing customer data, technical constraints, and business context into decisions.
Primary focus
Problem discovery → spec → delivery → outcome measurement
Authority ceiling
Feature-scope decisions — what this feature is, when it ships, whether it worked
Technical layer adds
Feasibility checks, existing system surveys, technical spec authorship, architecture-aware build sequencing — the load-bearing technical contribution
What differentiates from Sr. Manager
Operates within a strategy set above them — prioritization is intra-feature, not cross-feature portfolio allocation
Weight profile:
🔍 InsightOwns 📐 DefinitionOwns 🛠️ DeliveryOwns 🚀 LaunchOwns 📊 AnalyticsOwns 📣 CommunicationOwns 🎯 StrategyShares ⚙️ OperationsLight
★
Technical Sr. Manager
Owns strategy across features
Tier 3
The Sr. Manager operates at portfolio scope. They own the bets — which feature areas the team invests in, how the roadmap is framed to leadership, and whether to iterate, kill, or scale what's been built. Individual features are reviewed and unblocked, not owned.

The key shift from PM: authority is cross-feature and upward-facing. The Sr. Manager frames the product narrative for leadership, makes cross-team alignment calls, and decides the direction across an entire domain — not just one feature at a time.
Primary focus
Strategy, roadmap framing, outcome decisions (iterate / kill / scale)
Authority ceiling
Portfolio-level direction, cross-team priority calls, leadership narrative
Technical layer adds
Architecture standards across features, build-vs-buy decisions, tech-debt direction — technical strategy, not tactical spec work
What differentiates from PM
Sets the strategy PMs execute within — makes the bets, frames the narrative, resolves cross-team blockers through standing rather than direct ownership

The Delta Model

The three tiers are not separate job families — they are cumulative expansions of scope and authority. Each tier adds a new ceiling:

Coordinator → PM: gains problem ownership and spec authority. Stops producing inputs; starts making feature-level decisions.
PM → Sr. Manager: gains portfolio scope and leadership voice. Stops owning individual features; starts setting the direction features operate within.

The Technical overlay follows the same pattern. At Coordinator it is literacy and upkeep. At PM it becomes the load-bearing contribution (feasibility, technical spec, build sequencing). At Sr. Manager it shifts to technical strategy — architecture standards and make-or-buy calls that span the whole portfolio.
Implication for the playbook: a play that exists at the Coordinator tier but not the PM tier is not "missing" at PM — it has been delegated down. The Coordinator runs the machine so the PM can focus on the problem and the spec. Absence of a play at a higher tier is intentional: it signals that the work belongs to a lower tier, not that it doesn't exist.

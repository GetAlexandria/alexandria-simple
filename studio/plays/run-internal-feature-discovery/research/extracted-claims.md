## Section 2 — Teresa Torres (Continuous Discovery Habits; producttalk.org)

Sources: producttalk.org/2024/04/story-based-customer-interviews/ [F];
userinterviews.com/blog/how-to-interview-customers-continuously-with-teresa-torres [F];
producttalk.org/2024/02/interview-snapshot/ [F]; producttalk.org/stakeholder-management/
[F-secondary, summarized]; evansamek.substack.com/p/summary-continuous-discovery-habits [F].

2.1  "Keep the interview grounded in specific instances of past behavior."
     [F] producttalk.org/2024/04/story-based-customer-interviews/ — verbatim.

2.2  Foundational interview prompt: "Tell me about the last time you watched
     Netflix" (not "Tell me about your experience on Netflix"). The specific-
     instance framing "generates dramatically richer responses with contextual
     details the interviewer wouldn't have thought to ask about directly."
     [F] producttalk.org/2024/04/story-based-customer-interviews/ — verbatim
     example and paraphrase.

2.3  "When collecting stories, we want the participant to do most of the talking."
     [F] producttalk.org/2024/04/story-based-customer-interviews/ — verbatim.

2.4  Torres defines the target output of an interview as discovering "unmet customer
     needs, pain points, and desires. I collectively call these opportunities."
     [F] producttalk.org/2024/04/story-based-customer-interviews/ — verbatim.

2.5  Excavation technique — four specific methods:
     (a) Explicit invitation: invite participants to share their full story.
     (b) Temporal sequencing: "What happened first?" / "What happened next?"
     (c) Supporting context: ask about other people involved.
     (d) Specificity correction: when participants generalize, counter with
         "In this specific example, what did you do?"
     [F] evansamek.substack.com/p/summary-continuous-discovery-habits

2.6  "You never want to interrupt your interview participant. And even if what
     they're telling you is totally irrelevant, they're telling you it because they
     care about it." [F] userinterviews.com/blog/how-to-interview-customers-
     continuously-with-teresa-torres

2.7  "Tell me about a time when" questions work best because they "collect a
     specific story because that's what's going to be reliable feedback."
     [F] userinterviews.com/blog (same article) — paraphrase of Torres, not
     verbatim from book.

2.8  Research-question distinction: "Distinguish between what you are trying to
     learn (your research questions) from what you ask in the interview (your
     interview questions)." [F] evansamek.substack.com

2.9  Torres advises against telling participants in advance what topic will be
     discussed, because pre-warning causes them to craft their "best self" story
     rather than the real, messy one. [S] Search-snippet summary of Torres
     podcast content. ~~SNIPPET-ONLY — flagged for verification pass.~~
     [UNVERIFIED 2026-06-12] The specific producttalk.org URL for this claim

2.10 Opportunities must come from what customers actually said: "When we generate

2.11 Interview snapshot fields: participant name/photo, quick facts (role and

## Section 3 — NNGroup: interview question mistakes

Source: nngroup.com/articles/interview-questions-mistakes/ [F]

3.1  Mistake 1 — Starting with closed questions. Open-ended prompts like "Tell me
     about a time when you cooked a meal for yourself" allow participants to share
     stories and build rapport. [F] nngroup.com/articles/interview-questions-mistakes/

3.2  Mistake 2 — Asking only about typical behaviors. "What people typically do...
     and what they think they typically do may be different things!" Ask about
     specific examples instead. [F] nngroup.com — verbatim.

3.3  Mistake 3 — Asking hypothetical questions. "People are bad at predicting their
     future behavior." [F] nngroup.com — verbatim.

3.4  Mistake 4 — Using leading clarifications. Avoid framing interpretations as
     questions, especially those using "because." Instead of "Did you choose that
     because it was easy?", ask "Tell me why you chose that meal." [F] nngroup.com

3.5  Mistake 5 — Asking compound questions. These force participants to hold
     multiple questions in working memory; keep questions "short and concise."
     [F] nngroup.com — quote verbatim.

3.6  Mistake 6 — Asking ambiguous questions. Overly broad questions cause
     confusion; pilot the guide with participants to catch misinterpretations.
     [F] nngroup.com

3.7  Group stakeholder settings undermine candor — conducting interviews in group
     settings rather than one-on-one suppresses honest responses. [F — from prior
     research cycle on elicit-business-context] Confirmed at nngroup.com/articles/
     stakeholder-interviews/ — carried here as cross-referenced prior finding.

## Section 4 — JTBD switch interview (Moesta / Spiek)

Sources: thehuman2ai.com/research/guides/jtbd-switch-interview [F];
jobstobedone.org/learn/ [F-secondary, rendered];
deployempathy.substack.com/p/customer-interview-script-template (JTBD script) [F].

4.1  The six-stage buying timeline in the switch interview: (1) first thought —
     initial realization that change is needed; (2) passive looking; (3) active
     looking; (4) deciding; (5) onboarding; (6) ongoing use. [F] thehuman2ai.com

4.2  The four forces of progress: F1 push (frustration with current situation),
     F2 pull (attraction to new solution), F3 anxiety (fear of change), F4 habit
     (comfort with status quo). A switch occurs when F1 + F2 > F3 + F4.
     [F] thehuman2ai.com; corroborated by jobstobedone.org/learn/

4.3  Key interview technique: ask about the "first thought" moment — "when did they
     first realize their current solution might not be enough?" Often triggered by
     a specific event — a failed outcome, a frustrating experience, a change in
     circumstances. [F] thehuman2ai.com; also corroborated in search snippet from
     qualz.ai.

4.4  Strong vs. weak JTBD questions: Good: "Walk me through the day you decided
     to sign up." Weak: "What features made you choose us?" [F] thehuman2ai.com

4.5  Interview probing techniques: ask about weather, location, device to trigger
     recall; "listen for emphasis, sighs, pauses" and probe emotional moments;
     "play dumb — frame challenging questions as personal confusion"; use Five Whys.
     [F] thehuman2ai.com — paraphrased from fetched page.

4.6  JTBD script opening (Deploy Empathy template): "Hi, is this [person]? Thank you
     for taking the time to talk to us today." Before starting, confirm the purpose
     is to understand experience, not to evaluate. "Imagine that I'm filming a
     documentary. I'm just trying to understand how people buy/experience [area].
     Anything you can tell me here is going to be useful." [F] commoncog.com/
     putting-jtbd-interview-to-practice/ — verbatim from fetched page.

4.7  JTBD success measurement: if you cannot fill in the JTBD timeline template
     after the interview, the interview failed. [F] commoncog.com (same article)

4.8  "Interviews always veer off track...in fact, that is often the sign of a great
     interview!" — don't force script order; let respondents wander naturally.
     [F] deployempathy.substack.com/p/customer-interview-script-template

## Section 5 — Customer dev labs / Wilcox five-question script

Source: customerdevlabs.com/2013/11/05/how-i-interview-customers/ [F]

5.1  Five-question discovery script:
     1. "What's the hardest part about [problem context]?"
     2. "Can you tell me about the last time that happened?"
     3. "Why was that hard?"
     4. "What, if anything, have you done to solve that problem?"
     5. "What don't you love about the solutions you've tried?"
     [F] customerdevlabs.com — verbatim questions.

5.2  Critical Rule 1: "Do not talk about your idea." [F] customerdevlabs.com —
     verbatim.

5.3  Critical Rule 2: "Do not ask about the future." Never use the word "would."
     [F] customerdevlabs.com — verbatim.

5.4  "If they aren't looking for solutions already, this isn't a big enough problem
     for us to solve." [F] customerdevlabs.com — verbatim.

5.5  "All I have to do is listen. No pitching, no negotiating, just ask a handful of
     questions with a sincere interest to learn." [F] customerdevlabs.com — verbatim.

5.6  When someone gives a solution instead of a problem: if you must introduce the
     problem and they show no active attempt to solve it, you have invalidated your
     hypothesis. [F] customerdevlabs.com — paraphrase.

## Section 7 — Discovery interview failure modes (cross-source)

Sources: frwrdx.ai/post/how-to-run-customer-discovery-interviews [F];
craftuplearn.com/blog/product-discovery-interview-script-unbiased-insights [S];
pierre-fournier.medium.com/the-art-of-interviewing-in-product-discovery-cc95b67f2eeb [F]

7.1  "Most customer discovery interviews end with the founder feeling encouraged
     and having learned almost nothing." [F] frwrdx.ai — verbatim.

7.2  The confirmation exercise failure mode: founders approach interviews seeking
     validation, ask leading questions, describe solutions while asking about
     problems, and misinterpret courtesy as genuine interest. [F] frwrdx.ai —
     paraphrase.

7.3  "A yes that requires nothing is not a signal. A yes that costs the person
     something — time, a connection, a commitment — is." [F] frwrdx.ai — verbatim.

7.4  Four anti-patterns and remedies (from frwrdx.ai): hypothetical questions →
     remedy: ask about the last time they experienced the problem; leading with
     the product → remedy: open with the problem; ignoring current behavior →
     remedy: listen for workarounds; asking for costless commitments → remedy: end
     with asks requiring real investment. [F] frwrdx.ai

7.5  Confirmation bias "ruins your Discovery effort" by making interviewers
     influence users toward expected answers and miss contradictory signals.
     Mitigation requires pairing interviews and pre-interview self-awareness.
     [F] pierre-fournier.medium.com

7.6  Three language patterns that require interrogation: generalizations ("never,"
     "always"), deletions (vague statements like "it was okay"), distortions
     (false causal connections). [F] pierre-fournier.medium.com

7.7  Leaving silences when users process memories (indicated by eye movement) and
     rephrasing/mirroring their exact words back to them both draw out fuller
     stories. [F] pierre-fournier.medium.com — paraphrase.

7.8  "PMs must first put aside their ego because let's be honest with you dear PMs,
     we don't care about your brilliant assumptions, only do we care about our the
     user's context." [F] pierre-fournier.medium.com — verbatim (note: contains
     apparent typo in original: "our the user's context").

## Section 9 — Internal stakeholder interviews (User Interviews field guide)

Source: userinterviews.com/ux-research-field-guide-chapter/internal-stakeholder-interviews [F]

9.1  Internal stakeholder interviews serve four functions: define goals, understand
     limitations and assumptions, earn trust, gather existing knowledge.
     [F] userinterviews.com — paraphrase.

9.2  Format guidance: "Try not to read off a list of questions. Keep your list of
     must-asks short (2-3 questions) and memorize them. This should feel like a
     conversation, not an interrogation." [F] userinterviews.com — verbatim.

9.3  When stakeholders propose solutions: acknowledge their expertise while
     refocusing on understanding user problems — "Don't be dismissive of stakeholder
     concerns or skepticisms." [F] userinterviews.com — paraphrase.

9.4  Analysis: synthesize across interviews to identify patterns rather than being
     "swayed" by individual compelling voices. [F] userinterviews.com — near-verbatim.

## Section 11 — 80/20 talk ratio and one-question discipline

Sources: kromatic.com/blog/how-asking-works-a-crash-course-in-customer-discovery-questions [S];
customerdevlabs.com [F]; torres sources above.

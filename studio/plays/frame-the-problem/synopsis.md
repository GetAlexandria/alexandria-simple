## What it does

Frames the problem(s) underneath something you hand in — a solution you're
holding, a rough problem statement, a conversation — and produces one
co-edited document you shape together with it, the way you'd edit a Google doc
side by side. Two layers run it: **Raven is the face**, the agent you talk to
directly; **the play is her backstage**, the worker nodes that hand her the
material and the lines to draw out, like an actress fed by the writers' room.
The framing is built in service of facts and logic, not your solution — it may
end up supporting, complicating, or undercutting what you brought, and it only
backs your solution where a specific past instance earns it. A claim counts as
evidence only when it's anchored to a real, recent time it happened; on a
hand-wavey claim, the move is the open question "Can you tell me about a
specific time that happened recently?"

## Reach for it when

- someone handed you a **solution** and you want the problem(s) under it
- you're **pressure-testing** whether a solution is actually solid
- you're **sharpening a rough problem statement** into something specific
- you want a **problem framing you can co-edit fast** — react, watch it
  update, react again

## The story

The director hands in a feature he's calling the "dominos delivery tracker": a
food-delivery-style screen for long-running plays, with a time estimate as the
novel part he wants to crack. Raven doesn't ask him to explain it from a blank
page. Backstage drafts a first framing for her to react to, and she puts it in
front of him: two problems underneath the feature — losing your place during a
twenty-minute run, and not knowing when it'll finish — with the time-estimate
need flagged as thin, hypothetical, maybe just a bolt-on from the pizza
analogy.

He reacts with a specific time. Last Thursday around 4pm he kicked off a play,
pinged the agent twice because the chat went quiet, burned tokens doing it,
then alt-tabbed away for 25 minutes — and both times what he actually wanted to
know was whether he was 5 minutes or 25 minutes out. The framing folds it in:
the time question gets re-marked first-hand, the hierarchy flips so it's the
primary pain, and step-level visibility drops to secondary, in service of the
time question. A couple of passes later it reads as ready, and he approves.

## Trigger

**⚡ You hand Raven something to frame** — a solution pitch, a rough problem
statement, notes, a conversation. That's the cue. Backstage reads what you
handed in and drafts a framing to react to, rather than starting from a blank
question. If the material has nothing framable in it — logistics, chatter — it
says so plainly and asks whether you meant to hand over something else, rather
than inventing a problem to be helpful.

## Teresa Torres — opportunity solution trees / opportunity mapping
Sources: producttalk.org (primary): /opportunity-solution-trees/, /opportunity-mapping/, /prioritize-opportunities/, /glossary-discovery-opportunity-mapping/

- The opportunity solution tree is a four-layer structure: desired outcome →
  opportunities → solutions → experiments.
- An "opportunity" is broader than a "problem": an unmet need, pain point, or
  desire (products can serve desires without fixing problems).
- **Solution-in-disguise test:** ask whether there's more than one way to
  address it — if not, it's a solution, not an opportunity.
- Tangled problems are structured as a tree: experience map from customer
  stories → key moments become top-level opportunities → individual
  opportunities grouped under the moment they occur in.
- Evidence standard: opportunities must come from what customers actually said,
  not team brainstorming (self-generated opportunities import team bias).
- Opportunities are framed in the customer's voice ("I couldn't find anything
  to watch"), never as business desires ("I wish users binged more").
- Flat lists are rejected because opportunities vary in size, interrelate, and
  nest; the tree's relations are parent/child (child = subset of parent; child
  is a *reason for* its parent) and sibling (distinct, non-overlapping,
  independently addressable).
- Purpose of the mapping: decompose one intractable problem into smaller,
  individually solvable ones.

## Rob Fitzpatrick — The Mom Test (evidence standards)
Sources: momtestbook.com (primary); full text PDF (inkubator.si)

- Premise: direct feedback about an idea is systematically unreliable
  ("everyone is lying to you"); structure conversations to learn anyway.
- Three rules: talk about their life, not your idea; ask about concrete past
  specifics, not generics/opinions/the future; talk less, listen more.
- Evidence rules of thumb: "Opinions are worthless"; "Anything involving the
  future is an over-optimistic lie."
- Three types of bad data: compliments; fluff (generics, hypotheticals,
  futures); ideas. Remedy: anchor fluff to specific past instances ("When's
  the last time that happened?"). Most dangerous sentence: "I would definitely
  buy that."
- Proof a problem is real = **commitment** (giving up time, reputation, or
  money); a compliment costs nothing and carries no data.
- Solution-reversal: ask "Why do you bother?" to get from the perceived problem
  to the real one; test significance by implications ("some problems don't
  actually matter") and by past solution-seeking ("if they haven't looked for
  ways of solving it already, they're not going to buy yours"). "People know
  what their problems are, but they don't know how to solve them."
